Total taxable pay is everything the £100,000 limit looks at: base salary, bonus, shares that vest and taxable benefits such as private medical insurance. Your employer's pension percentage usually applies to base salary only, so put that in as pensionable salary. Benefits such as private medical insurance count for income tax but not National Insurance, so including them slightly overstates your National Insurance and student loan.

You can have both. A postgraduate loan is repaid on top of an undergraduate one, at 6% of income over £21,000. Undergraduate loans are 9% over their own threshold. Plan 5 repayments only exist from 2026-27.

2. Children

Your £500 Tax-Free Childcare top-up limit per child runs in 3-month blocks counted from the month you opened the account. Not sure, or no account yet? Leave it on April.

3. Pension

From April 2029, only the first £2,000 a year of pension salary sacrifice stays free of National Insurance. The rest pays National Insurance like other pension contributions. Income tax relief and your employer's contributions are not affected. Student loan repayments are assumed not to be affected.

4. Your break-even point

Up to this salary, getting under £100,000 through your pension leaves you with more take-home pay now and a bigger pension. Above it, each extra pound you put in costs you take-home pay today. It depends on your children's ages, your childcare costs, your student loan and your pension, and it falls as your children grow up and there is less childcare help to lose.

5. What is putting money into a pension worth to you?

Money you pay into your pension through your employer comes off your pay before tax. If that brings your income down to £100,000, you also keep childcare help worth thousands of pounds a year.

So up to a point you end up with more take-home pay now and a bigger pension. That is the break-even point shown above. Past it, every extra pound you put in costs you some take-home pay today, in return for money you cannot touch until retirement. However, even past the break-even point, if you value a bigger pension more than the take-home pay you give up in return, it can still be worth it to you.

There is no right answer. It depends on how tight money is now and how much you want for later. Your answer below sets the line the rest of this page works to.

Past the break-even point, how much less take-home pay a month would you accept to put money into your pension?

£100 a month is £1,200 a year. This only matters once you are past the break-even point. Before it, putting money in leaves you better off anyway.

Before you rely on this

  • Pension money is locked away until retirement. Count it only if you do not need the cash.
  • Funding and Tax-Free Childcare are checked against your expected income every 3 months, not once a year.
  • Both parents must be at or under £100,000 for the 30 hours and Tax-Free Childcare.
  • Tax-Free Childcare also needs each parent to expect to earn at least 16 hours a week at minimum wage, and you cannot get it while you receive Universal Credit (gov.uk). This calculator assumes both are fine.
  • Years after 2026-27 use 2026-27 tax figures and are estimates.
  • From April 2029, only the first £2,000 a year of pension salary sacrifice stays free of National Insurance (gov.uk). Student loan repayments are assumed not to be affected.
  • Unpaid leave and fewer hours also lower an employer pension that is a percentage of salary. Check how your scheme works.
  • Childcare is assumed to start at the age you enter for each child. Funded hours start in the term after 9 months.
  • This is general information, not financial advice.
